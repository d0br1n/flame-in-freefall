**Author:** John Uri, NASA Johnson Space Center
**Published:** 23 February 2022

## What it is

A NASA history article on the fire aboard the Russian space station Mir on 24 February 1997, which the article calls the worst in-orbit fire incident in the history of spaceflight. It is the only resource on the list describing a real fire in a crewed spacecraft in microgravity.

## Key facts

### Setting

- Mir normally housed three people. During a crew handover from 12 February to 3 March 1997 there were six: Valeri Korzun and Aleksandr Kaleri (ending a long mission), NASA astronaut Jerry Linenger, newly arrived Vasili Tsibliyev and Aleksandr Lazutkin, and Reinhold Ewald of the German space agency DLR.
- Normal oxygen supply came from two **Elektron** units, which split water by electrolysis. With a larger crew, **solid fuel oxygen generators (SFOG**, also called Vika or TGK) supplemented them. These burn canisters of solid lithium perchlorate to release oxygen; each canister supports one person for one day. The technology had been used for decades on Soviet and Russian submarines and had been used more than 2,500 times on Mir without incident.

### The fire

- Shortly after dinner, Lazutkin activated an SFOG canister in the **Kvant-1 module** and it ignited.
- It produced a **flame about three feet long**, throwing sparks and bits of molten metal across the module, with a large quantity of **dense smoke**.
- The flame **blocked the route to one of the two Soyuz escape vehicles**, the one Korzun, Kaleri and Ewald would have used.
- The crew put on oxygen masks. Korzun fought the fire with **foam extinguishers** (three in total) while Linenger held him in place, since there is nothing to brace against in weightlessness.
- The fire lasted several minutes and finally went out by itself; Korzun's efforts prevented significant damage, though some Kvant-1 panels were charred. The station structure was not damaged.
- Life support cleared the smoke over several hours. No crew member suffered lasting harm.

### Cause

- The damaged generator was returned on the next shuttle flight, but the damage made analysis difficult.
- After two years, investigators concluded that hydrocarbon contamination of the canister raised the risk, and that the most likely culprit was **latex glove material accidentally left in the canister** during ground preparation.

### Lessons learned and changes

- Changes to crew emergency training and to how ground controllers support a crew during such an event.
- Changes to ground preparation of SFOG canisters.
- The ISS has an oxygen system with dissimilar redundancy: three improved Elektron units in the Russian segment and the electrolysis-based Oxygen Generation System in the U.S. segment. An SFOG system with added safety measures remains available as a backup or for larger crews.
- The article says the lessons were passed on to ISS design and operations.

## How it relates to the challenge

This incident touches all three fire safety pillars in an actual orbiting habitat.

| Aspect of the Mir fire | Pillar | Related PSI investigations |
|---|---|---|
| Contaminant in an oxygen-producing device | Prevention | BASS, BASS-II, Saffire (material flammability); ACME CFI-G (high oxygen) |
| Dense smoke filling modules; hours to clear | Detection | SAME, SAME-R, DAFT, PSI-115 (smoke modeling) |
| Foam extinguishers of limited effect on an oxygen-fed flame | Suppression | FLEX, FLEX-2 (inert-gas suppression), BASS (nitrogen suppression) |
| Blocked escape route, awkward firefighting in weightlessness | Operations | Saffire (large-scale fire growth in a real spacecraft) |

It is also a case where the fuel supplied its own oxygen, so dilution or smothering, the suppression approaches FLEX studied, would have had limited effect. That is a useful limit to note when interpreting suppression data.

## Caveats

- The article is narrative history. It contains no quantitative data on smoke concentration, oxygen level or temperature.
- The self-oxidizing nature of the fuel makes this fire unlike the solid, liquid and gas fires in PSI; treat connections as thematic.
