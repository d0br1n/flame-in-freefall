# Resource 6: Apollo 13 (CSM-109, LM-7)

**Link:** https://sma.nasa.gov/sma-disciplines/accllp/apollo-13
**Type:** NASA historical and lessons-learned page
**Owner:** NASA Office of Safety and Mission Assurance, Apollo Challenger Columbia Lessons Learned Program (ACCLLP)
**Read:** 2026-11-14

## What it is

NASA's lessons-learned page on the Apollo 13 oxygen tank explosion, with sections on history, crew, mission, accident, investigation, lessons learned and tributes. The challenge page lists it as an incident showing the danger of fire and oxygen hazards in flight.

## Key facts

### The mission

- Planned as the third lunar landing, targeting the Fra Mauro region, in April 1970.
- Crew: Commander Jim Lovell, Lunar Module Pilot Fred Haise, and Command Module Pilot Jack Swigert, who replaced Ken Mattingly days before launch after a measles exposure.
- Early signs of trouble: ground tests had pointed to a previously damaged oxygen tank number two, and one second-stage engine shut down early during launch.

### The accident

- On the third day, shortly after a 49-minute TV broadcast, the crew heard a bang. **Oxygen tank number two in the Service Module had exploded**, and tank number one was damaged as well.
- Two of three fuel cells were lost within seconds; the spacecraft was seen venting oxygen. Loss of the tanks meant loss of electrical power and water.
- The landing was abandoned. With about 15 minutes of power left in the Command Module, the crew moved into the Lunar Module Aquarius as a lifeboat and looped around the Moon.
- Problems on the way home: limited power and water (six ounces per person per day), cabin temperature down to 38 °F with condensation on the walls, and the need to adapt the Command Module's square lithium hydroxide canisters to the Lunar Module's round fittings to scrub the air.
- Mission control had three days to write Command Module power-up procedures that would normally take three months. A concern was that condensation would short the electronics; the improved wiring insulation introduced after Apollo 1 prevented arcing.
- The crew splashed down in the Pacific on 17 April 1970.

### The investigation

- The Apollo 13 Review Board, chaired by Edgar M. Cortright, reported on 15 June 1970 after seven weeks of work by four panels. It attributed the accident to a combination of mistakes and a deficient design.
- **Root cause chain:** in 1965 the permissible voltage for the oxygen tank heaters was raised from 28 to 65 volts DC, but the heaters' thermostatic switches were not changed to match. During a final launch-pad test the heaters were left on for an extended period and reached about 1,000 °F, **degrading the Teflon insulation on wiring inside the tank**. After roughly eight hours of overheating the tank was vulnerable the next time it was filled with oxygen.

### Lessons learned and changes

- Redesign and recertification of the Service Module oxygen storage system, and a review of every Apollo system using cryogenic oxygen.
- For Apollo 14 onward: a third oxygen tank in a different bay, upgraded thermostats, removal of the stirring fans, and all electrical wiring sheathed in stainless steel.
- Stronger management and organization so that engineering and manufacturing processes of subsystems are properly understood.

## How it relates to the challenge

Apollo 13 illustrates **prevention through control of ignition sources and oxygen systems**: damaged insulation plus an electrical source inside a pure-oxygen vessel. It also shows how a fire or explosion far from Earth becomes a life-support emergency with no quick return, the situation Moon and Mars crews will face. It is less a "cabin fire" case than Apollo 1 or Mir, so its link to the PSI experiments is indirect: material behavior in high oxygen, and the value of predictive models.

| Apollo 13 factor | Related PSI investigations |
|---|---|
| Material (insulation) behavior in high-oxygen environments | ACME CFI-G, ACME BRE, BASS-II |
| Predicting ignition and flammability limits | FLEX, FLEX-2, BASS, Saffire |

## Brainstorming hooks

- A "distance from help" framing for rankings: weight findings by how much they matter when evacuation is impossible (Moon base, Mars transit).
- A causal-chain visual (design change → untested component → insulation damage → ignition in oxygen) as a template for explaining fire scenarios in the dashboard.
- Show how Apollo 1's lessons (wiring insulation) carried over and prevented a second failure, as an example of fire safety knowledge accumulating, which is what the dashboard is meant to speed up.

## Caveats

- The event was an explosion in a cryogenic oxygen tank, not a sustained flame in a crew cabin, and it sits close to the propellant and tank hazards that the challenge says are mostly outside PSI's scope. Use it as motivation, not as a direct data match.
- The page says the crew had to remove "carbon monoxide" from the Lunar Module; lithium hydroxide canisters remove carbon dioxide, so this is probably a wording error on the page.
- The Review Board report and Mission Report linked from the page were not read for this file.
