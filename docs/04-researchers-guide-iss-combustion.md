## What it is

A review of NASA's combustion research conducted on, or planned for, the ISS: past research, lessons learned, future opportunities, facilities, and how researchers get funding. The challenge page notes that basic information about most of the investigations it describes is in PSI.

## Structure

1. Unique features of the ISS research environment
2. Why conduct combustion research in microgravity?
3. Results from past and recent research: droplet combustion, gaseous diffusion flames, premixed flames, solid fuels and material flammability, super-critical processes
4. Highlights and lessons learned: flame existence, low-stretch flames, jet flames, flame stability, cool flames, ancillary hardware, spacecraft fire safety
5. Opportunities for combustion research on the ISS
6. Facilities and how to choose them
7. Developing and flying research on the ISS; what principal investigators should know
8. Funding opportunities and points of contact
9. Citations and appendix (mini-facility table, acronyms)

## Key points by topic

- **ISS environment:** roughly 400 km altitude, 51-degree inclination, 90-minute orbit.
- **Why microgravity:** buoyancy hides flame physics on Earth. Removing it allows simpler one-dimensional analysis and long experiments at realistic sizes, and it reproduces the conditions of a spacecraft fire.
- **Droplet combustion:** a spherical droplet inside a spherical flame is a benchmark geometry. Topics include radiative versus diffusive extinction, soot and multi-component fuels.
- **Gaseous flames:** jet flames show different heights and soot onset than on Earth.
- **Premixed flames:** "flame balls" were shown to exist, some of about 1 W burning for over 40 minutes.
- **Solid fuels:** radiation is a major factor; microgravity flammability can be lower than still-air tests suggest, yet ventilation flow can make flames stronger.
- **Super-critical water oxidation:** a candidate for life-support waste processing.
- **Highlights:** candle flames burned for over 45 minutes on Mir. **Cool flames** were found to keep burning after the visible flame went out in heptane and other alkane droplet tests.

## Spacecraft fire safety content (most relevant to the challenge)

- **Flammability:** some materials burn at lower oxygen levels in microgravity than in normal gravity. The safety margin assumed from still-air tests may not hold where ventilation strengthens flames.
- **Flow speed:** for flame spread over solids, the flammability minimum sits at a characteristic flow of about **10 cm/s**, which the guide notes is typical of spacecraft ventilation.
- **Flow direction:** at low flow in microgravity, flames can spread upstream, against the airflow, the opposite of normal-gravity behavior.
- **Ignition:** materials ignite more easily in low gravity because natural convection does not cool the surface. Aircraft tests suggest the critical ignition heat flux may be about half the normal-gravity value.
- **NASA-STD-6001 Test 1:** BASS results suggest this 1g upward flame propagation test may not be conservative for microgravity.
- **Suppression:** nitrogen aimed at a flame base does not always work; a jet can entrain air and strengthen the flame. BASS tested nitrogen suppression at 17% oxygen.
- **Detection:** smoke particles in microgravity differ in size from those on Earth, so detectors tuned for Earth could look for the wrong signal. A device measuring particulates and chemical species together outperformed aircraft smoke detectors in joint testing with the FAA.
- **Exploration atmospheres:** research covers reduced pressure with elevated oxygen.
- **Partial gravity:** BRE and a "Narrow Channel" 1g test method are intended to check whether current tests apply to low and partial gravity.
- **Three misconceptions the guide rejects:** that a steady fire cannot exist in still microgravity air; that a fire self-extinguishes without ventilation; and that terrestrial standards are overly conservative for space.

## Lessons learned and opportunities

- Keep experiments simple; complexity costs money and time.
- Design for flexibility and repair, with clear crew procedures; crew time is scarce, so experiments should run autonomously.
- Future directions listed: practical and surrogate fuels, high-pressure and spray combustion, turbulence, a microgravity wind tunnel for large solid samples in the MSG, and restoring ground-based drop-tower programs.
