## What it is

The home page of NASA's Combustion Science discipline at Glenn Research Center in Cleveland, Ohio. The challenge page describes it as basic information about most of the combustion investigations whose data is in PSI. It has four parts: an overview of why combustion is studied in microgravity, current experiments, archived experiments, and ISS facilities.

## Key content

### Why study combustion, and why in space

- Combustion provides about 85% of delivered energy in the United States and is part of many industrial processes. It also produces greenhouse gases and soot.
- After more than 80 years of research, even a kitchen stove is beyond detailed numerical modeling: real combustion involves hundreds of chemical species and thousands of reactions, and those determine flammability limits and pollutant emissions.
- On Earth, buoyancy dominates. A flame is around 2,300 K against roughly 300 K ambient, an eight-fold density change over about a centimeter. The resulting flow makes flames lift, point upward and flicker, which hides the physics of interest.
- Without gravity, buoyancy is suppressed and the problem can be reduced to much simpler one-dimensional systems. The ISS allows longer and larger experiments than drop towers or aircraft, and doubles as a test-bed for spacecraft fire risk.

### Current experiments

| Experiment | What the page says |
|---|---|
| **FM2** – Flammability of Materials on the Moon | The first combustion experiment on another planetary body; burns materials at the Moon's 1/6 gravity, building on ground (1g) and ISS (~0g) work. |
| **SoFIE** – Solid Fuel Ignition and Extinction | Fire growth and safety techniques in space. NASA manages fire risk through NASA-STD-6001, whose flame propagation test is run at 1g; some researchers believe that test is not conservative in low gravity. |
| **FLARE** – Flammability Limits At Reduced-g Experiment | Aims to correlate material flammability limits in normal gravity and microgravity, so that microgravity limits can be estimated from ground data. International team: JAXA, NASA, ESA, CNES, DLR and universities in Japan, France, Germany and the USA. |

### Archived experiments

| Experiment | One-line description from the page |
|---|---|
| **ACME** | Advanced combustion technology through fundamental microgravity research; main goal is better efficiency and lower pollutant emission in terrestrial combustion. |
| **BASS** | Burning and extinction characteristics of a wide variety of fuel samples in microgravity. |
| **DAFT** | Verified that a modified P-Trak, a key component of SAME, works properly in microgravity. |
| **FLEX** | Assesses the effectiveness of inert-gas suppressants in microgravity and seeks the most conservative estimate of the limiting oxygen index for steady combustion. |
| **Saffire** | Spacecraft fire safety demonstration, framed around crew safety as the first priority. |
| **SAME** | Spacecraft smoke detectors must detect different kinds of smoke: hydrocarbons produce soot, plastics produce droplets of recondensed polymer fragments. |
| **SLICE** | Coflow laminar diffusion flames, valuable because that configuration can be modeled accurately. |
| **SPICE** | Stability and extinction of jet diffusion flames, continuing the Laminar Soot Processes work from STS-94. |

### ISS facilities

- **Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR):** in the U.S. Destiny laboratory; a 100-liter combustion chamber; the only ISS rack dedicated to combustion. Developed at Glenn, launched November 2008 on STS-126.
- **Multi-user Droplet Combustion Apparatus (MDCA):** a multi-user insert for droplet combustion experiments that runs inside the CIR. Developed at Glenn, launched November 2008.
